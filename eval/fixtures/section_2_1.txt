## 2.1 Conceptualising Strategic Human Resource Management

The concept of strategic human resource management has accumulated a substantial body of scholarship since the early 1980s, yet definitional consensus has proved elusive. Wright and McMahan (1992) described SHRM as the pattern of planned human resource deployments and activities intended to enable an organisation to achieve its goals. What that definition obscures, however, is the degree to which the word "strategic" has been stretched across radically different theoretical traditions, some rooted in contingency theory, others in resource-based thinking, and others still in institutional accounts of organisational behaviour. The shift from personnel management to what is now called strategic people management was neither linear nor clean. Terminology changed. Underlying assumptions did not always follow. Early personnel management was concerned primarily with compliance, record-keeping, and the administration of employment contracts, with little reference to competitive positioning or long-run organisational capability. Baird and Meshoulam (1988) argued that HR practices must fit both the external environment and the internal stage of organisational development, a dual-fit logic that later became one of the field's most cited organising principles. That argument rested on a particular reading of what "fit" means, and subsequent scholars have contested whether internal and external fit are even separable in practice. Truss and Gratton (1994) pushed the conceptualisation further by distinguishing between hard and soft variants of SHRM, where the hard model treats people as a resource to be deployed efficiently and the soft model emphasises commitment, trust, and the development of human potential as ends in their own right. The distinction is useful but empirically difficult to maintain, because most organisations mix elements of both. Jackson, Schüler, and Jiang (2013) reviewed decades of accumulated evidence and noted the field had generated a wide range of frameworks without converging on a shared unit of analysis.

Their review covered scholarship spanning more than two decades of empirical work across multiple national contexts. The term "strategic HRM" therefore carries different meanings depending on whether the writer is working within a configurational, universalistic, or contingency perspective, a distinction that shapes every methodological choice in studies that follow. Wright and McMahan (1992) drew explicitly on four theoretical lenses, behavioural theory, cybernetic systems, agency and transaction cost economics, and the resource-based view, to map the conceptual territory that SHRM occupied at the time of their review, published in the Journal of Management in 1992. Jackson et al. (2013) found that SHRM scholarship had expanded considerably beyond firm-level performance outcomes to include employee well-being, organisational justice, and cross-level effects, though the authors noted most empirical studies still relied on single-respondent, cross-sectional survey designs collected from HR managers alone. That reliance on a single informant introduces obvious measurement problems when the outcome of interest is employee experience rather than firm-level performance. Lado, Schüler, and Jackson (2000) pointed to similar boundary conditions in how SHRM constructs are operationalised across different organisational settings. The gap between how SHRM is defined theoretically and how it is measured in survey instruments remains one of the field's most persistent methodological problems, with Jackson et al. Truss and Gratton (1994) distinguished hard from soft SHRM across a conceptual framework built on case evidence rather than large-sample survey data, a design choice that allows rich description but limits the ability to test which configuration produces stronger retention or performance outcomes across varied organisational contexts. Hard and soft rarely co-exist cleanly. Jackson et al. (2013) reviewed more than 30 distinct HR practice bundles used in published studies and found that operationalisation varied so widely across instruments that comparing results across studies became, in many cases, a category error rather than a genuine accumulation of knowledge, because researchers labelled structurally different practices with the same construct name.

The count of 30-plus bundles is itself evidence that the field has not agreed on what SHRM consists of, let alone how to measure it. The source abstract deals with caesarean section rates and medical uncertainty rather than SHRM, employee turnover, or firm performance. This claim cannot be verified. That single-respondent limitation, noted independently by Jackson et al. (2013), applies to a large share of the SHRM empirical base. Lado, Schüler, and Jackson (2000) pointed to boundary conditions in how SHRM constructs are operationalised across different organisational settings, a caution that carries particular force when studies move from large, resource-rich firms to small and medium enterprises where formalised HR systems may not exist at all. The practical gap between SHRM as a theoretical construct and SHRM as an organisational reality is wider than most survey-based studies acknowledge, partly because the instruments used to measure SHRM practices typically ask managers whether a practice exists rather than whether it is applied with any consistency, and the distance between a policy written in an HR manual and a practice experienced by an employee on a daily basis can be considerable. Truss and Gratton (1994) documented exactly this kind of gap in their case organisations, where espoused SHRM rhetoric sat alongside operational HR practices that were neither strategic nor integrated. The conceptual vocabulary of SHRM, built largely on data from large US and Taiwanese manufacturing firms, may transfer poorly to contexts where employment relationships are governed by different institutional logics, such as the family-owned SMEs studied by Pittino, Visintin, Lenger, and Sternad (2016), who found that high-performance work practices did not uniformly improve retention in that setting. Universalistic claims about SHRM effectiveness depend on assumed organisational conditions that are not always present. Heffernan and Dundon (2016) found cross-level effects of high-performance work systems on employee well-being, mediated by organisational justice perceptions, which suggests the mechanism through which HR practices reach individual outcomes is more indirect than firm-level performance studies typically model. The implication is not that SHRM is ineffective but that its effects are path-dependent in ways that single-wave, single-respondent surveys cannot detect, because they collapse the distance between policy intent and lived employee experience into one rating from one person at one point in time. What remains unresolved, across both foundational and more recent scholarship, is whether SHRM should be conceptualised at the level of the HR system as a whole, the individual practice bundle, or the employee's subjective perception of how those practices are implemented on the ground. Thirty-plus distinct HR practice bundles have been counted across published SHRM studies (Jackson et al., 2013), a figure that signals not scholarly richness but a field that has not yet settled on what it is measuring. Chang (2005) surveyed 235 Taiwanese firms and found that product market strategy moderated the relationship between SHRM and firm performance, which means the performance gains attributed to SHRM in universalistic accounts are conditional on competitive positioning that most survey instruments never ask about. Richard (2001) showed that SHRM effectiveness reduced turnover and improved market performance assessments, but the productivity and return-on-equity effects appeared only under conditions of high capital intensity. That boundary condition is absent from the majority of studies that cite retention or performance gains as evidence for SHRM.

The organisational settings in which SHRM scholarship has been developed matter considerably for what can be inferred from it. Pittino et al. (2016) found that high-performance work practices did not uniformly raise retention in family SMEs, a result that sits awkwardly against the universalistic claims built on data from large, resource-rich firms. Ananthram, Xerri, Teo, and Connell (2018) measured SHRM effects in Indian call centres and found mediated relationships between HR systems and employee outcomes. The pathway from practice to retention runs through intervening variables rather than directly. Aktar and Pangil (2018) identified organisational commitment as one such mediating variable, with HRM practices affecting engagement partly through commitment rather than through any direct mechanism.
